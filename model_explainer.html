How the two trade models work

A guided anatomy of two general-equilibrium models: the World Bank's practitioner tariff model (WP 11347), which we re-run unmodified from its released package, and our independent reconstruction of the OECD AI Meets Trade model (AI Papers No. 57), for which no code or data was released — what each one is, how it is calibrated, how a shock travels through it, and how the two behave when fed the same AI shock.

Orientation

Two models, one question each

Both are static, multi-country, multi-sector general-equilibrium trade models. Each starts from a snapshot of the world economy — who produces what, who buys from whom — and asks: if one thing changes, what new prices and trade flows make all markets clear again, and who ends up better or worse off?

They differ in the change they study. The World Bank model raises tariffs and traces the damage. The OECD model raises productivity — AI making workers and firms more efficient — and traces the gains, including how they leak across borders through trade.

Neither model is solved in levels. Both use "exact-hat algebra": instead of knowing every technology parameter and preference, they work entirely in proportional changes ("hats", x̂ = x′/x). Observed expenditure shares from the base-year data are sufficient statistics — the unobservable deep parameters cancel out.

That is why both calibrate to an inter-country input–output table: a giant accounting matrix recording every bilateral, sector-to-sector flow in the world economy for one year.

Anatomy

Side-by-side anatomy

World Bank · WP 11347

Purpose & pedigree

A practitioner's reference model: Eaton–Kortum (2002) trade with Caliendo–Parro (2015) input–output linkages, published with a verified reproducibility package in four languages. Its headline experiment is a ±25-percentage-point tariff war between high-income and non-high-income blocs.

OECD · AI Papers No. 57

Purpose & pedigree

A policy model of the global gains from AI: production networks with nested constant-elasticity-of-substitution ("CES") structure and origin-differentiated ("Armington") trade, in the Çakmaklı et al. / Baqaee–Farhi tradition. Its experiment is a decade of AI adoption raising sector productivity in every country at once — no official code was ever released, so this repository re-implements it from the paper's Annex A.

Production & trade structure

  • Cobb-Douglas everywhere: labour and intermediate inputs combine with fixed cost shares; consumers spend fixed shares across sectors.
  • Ricardian trade (EK): each sector is a continuum of varieties; countries draw productivities and the lowest-cost supplier wins each variety. One parameter — θ — governs how strongly trade shares respond to costs.
  • Tariffs drive a wedge between buyer and seller prices; the revenue is handed back to households lump-sum.

Production & trade structure

  • Nested CES: labour + capital form a value-added bundle (η = 0.6); that bundle combines with an intermediate bundle (φ = 0.6); intermediates across sectors are strong complements (ε = 0.2); within a sector, home and foreign varieties substitute with sector-specific Armington elasticities ξj.
  • Armington trade: goods are differentiated by origin country; trade shares respond to relative prices through ξj.
  • Consumption is Cobb-Douglas across sectors (σ = 1) with the same Armington structure within each sector.

Closure & welfare

  • Numeraire: world nominal gross output.
  • Trade imbalances: national savings fixed in numeraire units.
  • Aggregate labour fixed; its sectoral allocation responds to wage differentials (Weibull discrete choice, ν = 2).
  • Welfare read as income-based real income: nominal income deflated by the consumer price index — not expenditure-based, and not GDP.

Closure & welfare

  • Numeraire: world GDP ≡ 1.
  • Trade imbalances: fixed transfers in world-GDP units (sum to zero).
  • Factor incomes accrue domestically; population fixed.
  • Welfare is per-capita real income — nominal income over the exact cost-of-living index. The paper (and this repo) deliberately never calls it "GDP".

Solver

  • Damped fixed point on (expenditure, wages, input costs): guess, update, damp (ρ = 0.25/0.5/0.5), repeat.
  • Convergence: total error < 10⁻⁶, checked every 20 iterations; converges in ~220 iterations, 2.7 s on this machine.
  • Deterministic — byte-identical reruns; Python and R agree to 5.5×10⁻¹² with identical iteration paths.

Solver

  • Euler continuation: the decade of AI productivity is applied in N = 100 small slices; after each slice a sparse linearised equilibrium system is solved in factor-income space, shares are updated, and welfare is integrated along the path.
  • A Newton correction per step makes results N-independent to 1e-12; a direct nonlinear solve of the full system serves as the benchmark (agreement ~3×10⁻¹¹).
  • One full scenario: ~180 s, 1.5 GB peak, 3,465 goods × 3,517 factors.
The mathematics, in four lines

The equations that carry each model

Readable notation, hats denote proportional changes. Every equation below is mapped line-by-line to its implementing code in the repository's equation inventories.

Where the shock enters is a factor-of-two question. The OECD paper applies AI productivity to the value-added bundle (labour + capital), not to gross output. Because value added is roughly half of gross output (the world's gross-output-to-value-added ratio is ≈ 2.09), loading the same percentage on the wrong base doubles every result. This repository verified the value-added loading with a Hulten check: a uniform 0.1% VA-shock must raise world GDP by exactly 0.100% — and it does, to machine precision (gross loading would give 0.203%).

Pipeline validation wins. The occupation-level exposure aggregates re-derive from raw task ratings exactly (error ~10⁻¹⁷ — proving the paper's core-task weighting); the paper's top-5 most-exposed sectors reproduce in exact order (ICT, finance, publishing, professional services, telecoms); the adoption regression recovers R² 0.69 vs the paper's 0.74; and the US fast-adoption anchor (+57 pp over ten years) matches the paper's printed p. 69 statement to the letter.
Known reconstruction gaps — disclosed, and proven to matter. The paper's country-specific exposure uses restricted EU microdata (this repo substitutes US occupational structure), and far-tail adoption levels for some emerging economies reconstruct high. A controlled diagnostic proved the second gap is the binding one: replacing only the adoption input with the paper's own (properly re-measured) values closes ~98% of the top-five overshoot with every seeded country landing within ±10% — the GE mechanism itself is sound. Follow-up variants rebuilt 73 of 76 countries' occupation mixes from public and official statistics (Eurostat, ILOSTAT, and national census tables incl. Canada, Hong Kong, Taiwan and New Zealand), recovering most of the missing exposure ranking (ρ 0.80 → 0.92 vs the paper's own figure); China's cross-tab is verified unreachable from public data. A further rebuild of the adoption input on recovered 2024 published data (the "re-anchored" build) hits all 14 printed anchors and closes over half the remaining laggard gap — it is the designated successor, its promotion withheld only because one pre-registered anchor check (Switzerland's ceiling) fails by 1.4%. Exposure remains the second-order channel; the residual adoption gap is the dominant one.

The pure-Euler path shows textbook O(1/N) convergence (halve the step, halve the error); the Newton-corrected path is N-independent to 10⁻¹² and agrees with a direct nonlinear solve of the full 7,000-equation system to ~10⁻¹¹. Zero shock returns exactly the base year; scaling the numeraire changes nothing real. These are the invariants the test suite pins.

Cross-model experiment

Same AI shock, both models

As a capstone, the OECD A2 shock was harmonised onto the World Bank model's 81 × 21 grid and run through a clearly-labelled variant of its solver (tariffs untouched, one-line hat-algebra change). The first attempt looked like disagreement — the WB model produced roughly twice the OECD numbers. The cause was the factor-of-two units trap above: the OECD shock is a value-added concept, the WB model wanted gross-output units. Trust, but verify

How much to trust each model

TRACK AWorld Bank — computational replication, machine-precision.

The official package ran unmodified: all 464 published values reproduce (tables, figures, in-text anchors), outputs match the authors' reference files to 2.7×10⁻¹², and an independent R run agrees to 5.5×10⁻¹². "Exact replication" is deliberately not claimed — the calibration-construction stage (proprietary MATLAB/Stata + restricted tariff sources) was consumed as shipped, not re-executed.

TRACK BOECD — structural reconstruction, scoped quantitative claim.

With no official code or data, the model was rebuilt from the paper's equations. The headline OECD-core results land on the paper (US scenario effect 0.911 vs ~0.9 pp; foreign-gains median 0.024 vs ~0.03; scenario ranges bracket), and the cross-country ranking is faithful — but emerging-economy tails overshoot through one identified input, and spillover-scenario magnitudes run at ~⅓ strength. Every Singapore number carries this caveat. Classification: structural implementation; close quantitative reconstruction for the headline family only.

Process worth trusting. Eight independent audit gates plus a pre-registered promotion trial ran between specification and release. Gate 4 caught a 3% accounting wedge before a single scenario was solved; Gate 5 re-ranked the team's own error diagnosis by computation; Gate 7 recomputed ~60 headline claims from the artifacts (all matched); Gate 8 adjudicated the improvement cycle — and when a demonstrably better adoption build failed one of five promotion gates committed to git 99 seconds before the first solve (by 1.4%), promotion was withheld rather than the goalposts moved. No parameter was ever tuned to match the paper.
